# Metacognition and Learning — Mistake

## When it's worth the full process, and when it's not

- **Do it when:** you'll depend on this concept later — production code, an on-call shift, a system you'll own — not just a one-time answer to move past right now.
- **Do it when:** you notice you've skimmed the same doc two or three times and still can't explain it. That's a clear sign to stop rereading and switch method.
- **Skip the full process when:** you just need one fact to unblock yourself right now, like a function's exact argument order. Not every lookup needs to become a learning project.
- **The real cost:** building something yourself and coming back to it later both take more time up front than just skimming a doc once. It's worth that time only for ideas you'll actually reuse.

## Mistakes that come from feeling instead of testing

- **Mistaking "this feels familiar" for "I understand this."** Rereading a doc or rewatching a tutorial makes it feel smooth and easy, and that smooth feeling gets mistaken for real understanding — researchers call this the illusion of fluency.
  - Fix: close the material and try to explain or rebuild the idea before deciding you "get it."
- **Using rereading and highlighting as your main study method.** A large review of ten study techniques found rereading and highlighting are some of the weakest, even though they're the most common way people study.
  - Fix: replace them with testing yourself (retrieval) or explaining *why* something works (elaboration) — the same review rated both far higher.
- **Reaching for a complicated word instead of a plain one.** Using jargon to describe something can hide a fuzzy spot instead of showing real mastery.
  - Fix: if you can't say it in plain words, you don't understand it yet — that's the whole point of the Feynman technique.

## Mistakes about how much guidance to use

- **Skipping the guided example when a concept is truly brand new.** Jumping straight to "figure it out yourself" on something you have zero background in wastes time rediscovering things a five-minute example would have shown you right away.
  - Fix: for a genuinely new idea, follow one worked example first — it's the right amount of help for a beginner.
- **Staying on guided examples too long once you're not a beginner anymore.** The same research that shows worked examples help beginners also found they stop helping — and can even get in the way — once you have real background in that area.
  - Fix: once you're not starting from zero, move to building on your own sooner rather than following another tutorial.

## Mistakes about timing

- **Cramming everything into one sitting, right before you need it.** It feels efficient, but a single session mostly fades within days.
  - Fix: spread your practice across a few days instead of one long session, even though it feels slower.
- **Treating "I finished the tutorial" as "I learned it."** Finishing a guided example only proves you can follow someone else's steps — it hasn't tested whether you can produce the idea yourself yet.
  - Fix: the tutorial isn't done until you've built a small version alone, with it closed.
