# Delegation — Senior

**Your question:** How do I choose work that develops people, provide the right support without micromanaging, and build a team that can operate without me?

Middle level teaches you to delegate with ownership. At senior level, you're delegating strategically — choosing work that stretches people, providing the right kind of support, and building a team that can make good decisions independently. The goal is not just to get work done; it's to grow the next generation of leaders.

## Choosing work that develops people

Not all work is equally valuable for development. Choose work that:

1. **Stretches them.** It's harder than what they've done before, but not so hard they'll fail. If they've led a team of 3, leading a team of 8 is a stretch. Leading a team of 50 might be too much.

2. **Has clear success criteria.** They need to know what winning looks like. "Improve team velocity" is vague. "Reduce deployment time from 30 minutes to 10 minutes" is clear.

3. **Has real consequences.** If the work doesn't matter, they won't learn as much. Redesigning a critical system teaches more than refactoring a utility library.

4. **Exposes them to new skills.** If they've only done technical work, give them a project that requires stakeholder management. If they've only worked on one team, give them a cross-team initiative.

5. **Has a built-in feedback loop.** They should be able to see the results of their work quickly. A 6-month project with no feedback is harder to learn from than a 2-week project with weekly reviews.

## The method: stretch, support, and reflect

1. **Identify the stretch.** "You've led technical projects. This one requires managing stakeholders and trade-offs. That's new for you."

2. **Provide the right support.** Not micromanagement, but active support. "I'll help you navigate the stakeholder conversations. You own the technical decisions."

3. **Set up reflection.** "After each phase, let's talk about what you learned. What would you do differently?"

4. **Celebrate growth.** "You handled that conflict really well. You listened to both sides and found a solution that worked."

## Realistic scenario: Developing a tech lead

**The person:** A strong engineer who has never led a team. They're ready to grow.

**The work:** Lead the redesign of the payment system. It's a 3-month project that requires coordinating with 3 other teams, making trade-off decisions, and managing a complex migration.

**The stretch:** They've never had to negotiate with other teams or make decisions that affect multiple systems.

**The support:**
- Weekly 1-on-1s to discuss challenges
- You attend the first few stakeholder meetings to model how to handle disagreements
- You review their design decisions and ask questions to help them think through trade-offs
- You help them navigate a conflict between teams about the migration timeline

**The reflection:**
- "What surprised you about leading this project?"
- "How did you handle the conflict between teams? What would you do differently?"
- "What did you learn about making trade-off decisions?"

**The outcome:**
- The project succeeds
- They've learned how to lead across teams
- They're ready for the next stretch: leading a larger initiative or managing a team

## Knowing when to step back

As people grow, you need to step back. Signs that someone is ready for more autonomy:
- They're making good decisions without your input
- They're handling conflicts and blockers independently
- They're thinking about the long-term impact of their work
- They're helping others grow

When you see these signs, give them more autonomy. "I'm going to step back from the weekly syncs. Let's do monthly instead. Come to me if you need help."

## Building a team that operates independently

The goal of delegation is not just to get work done — it's to build a team that can operate without you. This means:

1. **Clear decision rights.** People know what they can decide, what requires discussion, and what needs escalation.

2. **Shared mental models.** The team understands the strategy, the constraints, and the trade-offs. They can make decisions that align with the overall direction.

3. **Psychological safety.** People feel safe making decisions, failing, and learning. They don't need permission for everything.

4. **Feedback loops.** The team gets regular feedback on their decisions and outcomes. They learn from mistakes quickly.

5. **Distributed leadership.** Multiple people can step up and lead when needed. It's not all on you.

## Handling delegation failures

Sometimes delegation doesn't work out. The person gets stuck, the work goes off track, or they're not ready for the stretch.

**If they're stuck:**
- Ask what the blocker is. Often they know but haven't articulated it.
- Help them think through options, don't solve it for them.
- If they're truly stuck, step in and help, but make it a learning moment.

**If the work is going off track:**
- Catch it early. Don't wait until it's too late.
- Ask what changed. Often there's new information that changes the approach.
- Adjust the plan together, don't just take over.

**If they're not ready:**
- Be honest. "I think this stretch is too big right now. Let's find something that's challenging but achievable."
- Don't let them fail completely. That's not development; that's abandonment.
- Find a smaller stretch that builds toward the bigger one.

## Questions that expose weak assumptions

1. What would this person learn from this work?
2. What support do they need to succeed?
3. How will I know if they're struggling?
4. What's my role if things go wrong?
5. How will they reflect on what they learned?

1. How do you choose work that stretches someone without breaking them?
2. What's the difference between support and micromanagement?
3. How do you know when someone is ready for more autonomy?
4. What should you do if someone fails at a delegated task?
