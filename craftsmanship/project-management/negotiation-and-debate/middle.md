# Negotiation and Debate — Middle

**Your question:** How do I negotiate effectively? How do I convince people? How do I know when to say yes and when to say no?

Junior level teaches you to understand the other person and find common ground. At middle level, you're negotiating effectively, persuading people, and making clear decisions about what to commit to.

## Negotiating effectively

Good negotiation is not about winning. It's about finding a solution that works for both sides.

**The method: understand, propose, negotiate, agree, commit**

1. **Understand their position.** What do they want? What are their constraints?

2. **Propose a solution.** Based on what you know, propose something that might work for both sides.

3. **Negotiate.** Listen to their feedback. Adjust your proposal. Find middle ground.

4. **Agree.** Make sure you both agree on what you're committing to.

5. **Commit.** Follow through on what you agreed to.

## A concrete example: Negotiating a deadline

**Situation:** Your manager wants a feature done in 2 weeks. You think it needs 4 weeks.

**Bad approach:**
"I need 4 weeks. That's final."

Problems:
- Doesn't understand their constraint
- Doesn't look for middle ground
- Likely to escalate

**Good approach:**

**Understand their position:**
"I understand you want this done in 2 weeks. Can you tell me why? What's the business need?"

Manager: "We have a customer demo in 2 weeks. We need to show this feature."

**Understand your constraint:**
"I understand. I'm concerned that 2 weeks isn't enough time. Let me break down the work:
- Design: 3 days
- Implementation: 5 days
- Testing: 3 days
- Buffer for issues: 2 days
Total: 13 days, which is 2.6 weeks."

**Propose a solution:**
"What if we do this: we ship a basic version in 2 weeks for the demo. Then we add the advanced features in the following 2 weeks. That way, you have something to show the customer, and we have time to do it right."

**Negotiate:**
Manager: "Can you ship the basic version in 1.5 weeks?"

You: "That's tight, but possible if we cut some features. What if we cut the advanced filtering for now? We can add it later."

Manager: "OK, that works. But I need daily updates so I know we're on track."

**Agree:**
"OK, so we're committing to:
- Basic version (without advanced filtering) in 1.5 weeks
- Daily updates on progress
- Advanced features in the following 2 weeks
Is that right?"

Manager: "Yes."

**Commit:**
"Great. I'll start today and send you a daily update."

Better because:
- You understood their constraint (customer demo)
- You proposed a solution that works for both sides
- You negotiated and found middle ground
- You both agreed on what you're committing to
- You committed to follow through

## Convincing people

To convince people, you need to:

1. **Understand their perspective.** What do they care about? What are their concerns?

2. **Address their concerns.** Don't ignore them. Address them directly.

3. **Provide evidence.** Don't just say "this is good." Show why.

4. **Find common ground.** What do you both want?

5. **Make it easy to say yes.** Remove barriers to agreement.

## A concrete example: Convincing people to use a new technology

**Situation:** You want to use a new database technology. Your team is skeptical.

**Bad approach:**
"This technology is better. We should use it."

Problems:
- Doesn't address their concerns
- Doesn't provide evidence
- Doesn't make it easy to say yes

**Good approach:**

**Understand their perspective:**
"I know you're skeptical about this new database. What are your concerns?"

Team: "We don't know it. We'd have to learn it. It might have bugs. It might be abandoned."

**Address their concerns:**
"Those are valid concerns. Let me address them:
- Learning: It's similar to PostgreSQL, so the learning curve is not steep.
- Bugs: It has 50k GitHub stars and active maintenance.
- Abandonment: It's backed by a major company and has a large community.
- Performance: It's 10x faster for our use case based on benchmarks."

**Provide evidence:**
"Here are the benchmarks: [show data]
Here's the GitHub activity: [show commits and issues]
Here's the community size: [show stats]"

**Find common ground:**
"We both want a database that's fast, reliable, and maintainable. This technology gives us that."

**Make it easy to say yes:**
"What if we do a spike? We use it for one feature, see how it goes, and then decide whether to use it more widely. That way, we can test it without committing fully. If it doesn't work out, we can roll back."

Team: "OK, let's try it."

Better because:
- You understood their concerns
- You addressed each concern directly
- You provided evidence
- You found common ground
- You made it easy to say yes (spike, not full commitment)

## Saying yes and saying no

Saying yes and no clearly is important. Ambiguous yes/no creates confusion.

**Saying yes:**
- Be clear about what you're committing to
- Be clear about the conditions
- Be clear about the timeline
- Follow through

**Saying no:**
- Be clear about why you're saying no
- Offer an alternative if possible
- Be respectful
- Don't leave them hanging

## A concrete example: Saying yes and no

**Saying yes:**
"I can help with that. Here's what I'm committing to:
- I'll review the design by Friday
- I'll provide feedback on the implementation
- I'll help with testing
- I'll be available for questions during the implementation

Is that what you need?"

**Saying no:**
"I can't take on that project right now. Here's why:
- I'm committed to the payment system migration for the next 4 weeks
- I don't have capacity for another project

What I can do:
- I can help you find someone else who might be available
- I can review your plan and give feedback
- I can help after the migration is done

Does that work?"

Better because:
- Clear about what you're committing to (or not)
- Clear about why
- Offering alternatives
- Respectful and not leaving them hanging

## Common mistakes at middle level

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Not understanding their constraint | You propose something that doesn't work for them | Ask about their constraint |
| Proposing a solution without understanding | Your proposal misses the mark | Understand first, then propose |
| Not addressing their concerns | They don't agree | Address each concern directly |
| Not providing evidence | They don't believe you | Provide data, benchmarks, examples |
| Saying yes to everything | You overcommit and burn out | Say no when you need to |
| Saying no without offering alternatives | They feel rejected | Offer alternatives when possible |
| Being ambiguous about yes/no | They don't know what you're committing to | Be clear about what you're committing to |
