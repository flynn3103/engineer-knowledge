# Manage Up — Senior

**Your question:** How do I influence decisions? How do I escalate issues effectively? How do I guide my manager?

Senior level teaches you to influence decisions, escalate issues effectively, and guide your manager. The key is building trust and providing data-driven recommendations.

## Influencing decisions

Influence decisions by:

1. **Understand your manager's priorities.** What matters to them?

2. **Align with their priorities.** Show how your proposal helps them.

3. **Provide data and evidence.** Don't just give opinions.

4. **Propose multiple options.** Show trade-offs.

5. **Make a recommendation.** Be clear about what you think is best.

## A concrete example: Influencing decisions

**Situation:** You want to refactor the payment system. Your manager is focused on shipping features.

**Bad approach:**
"We should refactor the payment system. The code is messy."

Problems:
- Doesn't align with manager's priorities
- No data
- No clear recommendation

**Good approach:**

You: "I want to discuss the payment system. I know you're focused on shipping features. I think refactoring will actually help us ship faster.

Here's the data:
- Current payment system has 3 critical bugs per month
- Each bug takes 2 days to fix
- That's 6 days per month of unplanned work
- Refactoring will take 2 weeks, but will reduce bugs to 0.5 per month
- ROI: 2 weeks of work now saves 5.5 days per month going forward

Options:
1. Don't refactor. Keep dealing with bugs (6 days/month of unplanned work)
2. Refactor now. Invest 2 weeks, save 5.5 days/month going forward
3. Refactor gradually. Spend 1 day per week on refactoring

I recommend option 2. We'll be slower for 2 weeks, but faster long-term.

What do you think?"

Manager: "That makes sense. Let's do it. When should we start?"

Better because:
- You aligned with manager's priorities (shipping features)
- You provided data (bugs, time, ROI)
- You proposed options
- You made a clear recommendation

## Escalating issues effectively

Escalate issues by:

1. **Identify the issue.** What's the problem?

2. **Explain the impact.** How does it affect the business?

3. **Explain why you can't solve it.** Why do you need help?

4. **Propose a solution.** What should happen?

5. **Ask for help.** What do you need from your manager?

## A concrete example: Escalating issues

**Situation:** Your team is being asked to do too much. You're being asked to build features, fix bugs, and maintain infrastructure.

**Bad approach:**
"We're too busy. We need to stop doing something."

Problems:
- Vague
- No data
- No proposed solution

**Good approach:**

You: "I need to escalate an issue about our team's workload.

Here's the situation:
- We have 5 engineers
- We're asked to: build features (60%), fix bugs (20%), maintain infrastructure (20%)
- That's 100% utilization
- We have no time for code review, testing, or learning
- This is unsustainable

Impact:
- Code quality is declining
- Bugs are increasing
- Team morale is declining
- People are burning out

Why I can't solve it:
- This is a prioritization decision that needs to come from leadership
- I can't unilaterally reduce scope

Options:
1. Hire more engineers (takes 2-3 months)
2. Reduce feature scope (defer some features)
3. Reduce infrastructure maintenance (outsource or automate)
4. Reduce bug fixing (accept more bugs)

I recommend option 2 or 3. We need to reduce scope to make this sustainable.

What would you prefer?"

Manager: "I understand. Let me talk to the product team about reducing scope."

Better because:
- You identified the issue with data
- You explained the impact
- You explained why you can't solve it
- You proposed options
- You asked for help

## Guiding your manager

Sometimes you need to guide your manager:

1. **Understand their perspective.** What are they thinking?

2. **Provide information they might not have.** What do they need to know?

3. **Ask questions.** Help them think through the issue.

4. **Make a recommendation.** Be clear about what you think is best.

## A concrete example: Guiding your manager

**Situation:** Your manager wants to commit to a deadline that's unrealistic.

**Bad approach:**
"That deadline is impossible. We can't do it."

Problems:
- Confrontational
- Doesn't help your manager think through the issue

**Good approach:**

You: "I understand you want to commit to that deadline. Let me share some information that might help.

Here's what we know:
- The feature requires 20 days of work
- We have 2 engineers
- That's 10 days available
- We're 2x overloaded

Questions to consider:
- What if we miss the deadline? What's the impact?
- What if we reduce scope? What's the minimum viable version?
- What if we get help from another team? Is that possible?

My recommendation:
- Commit to a smaller scope by the deadline
- Commit to the full scope 2 weeks later

That way, we can launch something on time, and add features incrementally.

What do you think?"

Manager: "That makes sense. Let me talk to the product team about reducing scope."

Better because:
- You understood their perspective
- You provided information
- You asked questions
- You made a recommendation

## Building trust with your manager

Trust is the foundation of managing up:

1. **Do what you say you'll do.** Follow through on your commitments.

2. **Be honest about problems.** Don't hide issues.

3. **Provide good data.** Back up your recommendations with evidence.

4. **Respect their time.** Be concise and prepared.

5. **Support their goals.** Help them succeed.

## A concrete example: Building trust

**Over time:**
- You commit to things and deliver them
- You raise issues early
- You provide data-driven recommendations
- You're prepared for meetings
- You help your manager succeed

**Result:**
- Your manager trusts you
- Your manager listens to your recommendations
- Your manager advocates for you
- Your manager gives you more autonomy

## Common mistakes at senior level

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Not understanding manager's priorities | Your proposals don't align | Understand their priorities |
| Not providing data | Your recommendations aren't credible | Provide data and evidence |
| Being confrontational | Your manager gets defensive | Be collaborative |
| Not escalating issues | Issues get worse | Escalate when needed |
| Not building trust | Your manager doesn't listen | Do what you say, be honest, provide data |
