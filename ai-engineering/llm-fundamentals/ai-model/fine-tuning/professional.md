# Fine-Tuning — Professional

<!-- level-focus -->
At professional level, focus on this question:

> How do you run fine-tuning as a durable, org-wide operating model — with data provenance and privacy controls, a retraining cadence tied to drift signals, a rollback strategy, and honest cost accounting — so teams can ship and maintain custom fine-tuned models without a central team reviewing every training run?

Use the smallest realistic scenario that exposes the decision and its failure behavior.

---

## Core Concept 1 — Ownership Aligned to Cognitive Load

The predictable failure mode at scale: a central ML platform team tries to personally vet every team's training dataset, training run, and evaluation result before it ships, and becomes the bottleneck the moment more than a handful of teams are fine-tuning models. The split that scales distributes ownership by who actually has the context to make each decision correctly and sustain it over time:

| Layer | Owner | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Base/pretrained model selection and shared training infrastructure** | Platform/ML infra team | Maintain the approved list of base models, the training pipeline (job scheduling, GPU allocation, LoRA/QLoRA tooling), and the shared regression-suite tooling every team's fine-tune runs against |
| **Task-specific dataset curation and labeling** | The team that owns the product surface | They know the task, the label taxonomy, and what a correct output looks like — this cannot be centralized without losing domain context |
| **Data provenance and privacy review** | Data governance / privacy team | Define what training data requires review before use, own the PII-detection tooling, and sign off on datasets that include user-generated or otherwise sensitive content |
| **Evaluation policy and regression gates** | ML platform, in consultation with each product team | Define the shared regression-suite content and the acceptance threshold below which a fine-tune cannot ship, while task-specific metrics stay owned by the product team |
| **Program health, cost, and drift monitoring** | A governance working group spanning platform, data, and product | Track adoption, training/serving cost trends, and drift signals across all deployed fine-tunes; escalate when a model is overdue for retraining or review |

This keeps each layer within what its owner can actually sustain: no product team is expected to build its own PII-detection tooling from scratch, and no central team is expected to know every product surface's label taxonomy well enough to curate its dataset.

## Core Concept 2 — Data Provenance and Privacy

Before any dataset is used for training, the questions that must be answered and recorded, not assumed:

- **Where did each labeled example come from?** Historical production logs, human-written examples, or synthetic data generated by another model — each has a different provenance risk. Production-log-derived examples in particular need a documented origin, because "the training data" is now a system that has to be governed the same way any other data store containing user content is governed.
- **Does the dataset contain PII, and was that PII in scope of user consent for this use?** A support-ticket dataset drawn from real customer messages likely contains names, account details, or other identifying information. Using it to train a model requires the same review a new data product using that data would require — an existing "we're allowed to store this for support purposes" agreement does not automatically cover "we're allowed to use this to train a model," and the two need to be checked separately.
- **Can a specific individual's data be removed from a trained model if required?** Unlike a database row, a fine-tuned model's weights do not have an obvious per-example deletion mechanism — deleting a source record does not un-train the model. The practical answer at professional level is usually procedural, not technical: maintain the ability to retrain excluding a given record, track which model versions were trained on data including a since-deleted record, and have a documented process for when a deletion request requires a retrain versus when it doesn't.
- **Is there a retention policy for training datasets themselves**, separate from the retention policy for the source system the data was pulled from? A training dataset copy that outlives the retention period of its source system is a governance gap that an audit will find.

None of this is optional infrastructure — it's the difference between "we fine-tuned a helpful model" and "we have a model artifact whose training data we cannot account for," which is the kind of gap that turns into a serious problem exactly when it's least convenient: during an audit, a legal request, or a public incident.

## Core Concept 3 — Retraining Cadence Tied to Drift Signals

A fine-tuned model is not a one-time deliverable; production data drifts (Core Concept 5 in the senior guide), and a durable operating model needs a cadence that reacts to that, not a fixed calendar disconnected from actual need:

```yaml
retraining_triggers:
  scheduled_floor: "no fine-tuned model goes longer than N months without a re-evaluation, regardless of other signals"
  drift_signal: "input-distribution monitoring shows a new or growing category/pattern absent from training data, above an agreed threshold"
  regression_signal: "task-specific metric on a rolling sample of live traffic drops below the acceptance threshold"
  upstream_change: "a known product change (new feature launch, UI change, new user segment) that plausibly shifts the input distribution"
```

The scheduled floor exists because drift can be gradual enough that no single monitoring threshold trips, while the model still quietly degrades over a longer horizon than any one alert window covers. The drift and regression signals are the ones that should trigger retraining *before* the scheduled floor, so the floor is a backstop, not the primary mechanism — a team that only retrains on the fixed schedule and never on drift signals will consistently discover regressions the way the senior-level scenario did: from user complaints, weeks after the shift began.

## Core Concept 4 — Rollback Strategy

The rollback requirement is the same principle as the senior-level guide's Core Concept 6, formalized as policy rather than a per-team habit:

- **Every fine-tuned model version deployed to production has its predecessor (including the original pre-fine-tuning base model) kept deployable**, with a documented, tested procedure for reverting traffic to it — not just "the old model still exists in a registry somewhere."
- **A rollback does not require re-diagnosing the failure first.** The decision to roll back is "is the current model performing acceptably right now," answerable from monitored metrics; the decision to *retrain* is a separate, slower process that happens after rollback has already restored service.
- **Rollback is rehearsed, not assumed to work.** A rollback procedure that has never actually been executed outside an incident is a rollback procedure of unknown reliability — treat it the same way a disaster-recovery failover is treated: tested on a schedule, not only when needed for real.

## Core Concept 5 — Cost Accounting

A custom fine-tuned model has two cost components that a shared, off-the-shelf base model mostly avoids, and both need to be accounted for honestly against the alternative:

| Cost | What it covers | How it compares to a shared base model |
|---|---|---|
| **Training cost** | GPU time for the training run itself (a LoRA/QLoRA run is far cheaper than full fine-tuning — often a single GPU for hours rather than a multi-GPU cluster for days), plus the ongoing cost of dataset curation, labeling, and evaluation-suite maintenance | Zero for a shared base model used purely via prompting/RAG — this is the primary cost fine-tuning adds |
| **Serving cost** | Hosting a custom model (or adapter) — for a LoRA adapter, this can be as cheap as swapping a small adapter onto an already-hosted base model at inference time; for a fully separate full fine-tune, it may mean a dedicated deployment | LoRA/QLoRA adapters served on top of a shared base deployment add comparatively little serving overhead; full fine-tunes deployed as standalone models add a full model's worth of serving infrastructure, per fine-tuned variant |
| **Ongoing maintenance cost** | Retraining runs (Core Concept 3), regression-suite upkeep, provenance and privacy review on new data, drift monitoring | This is the cost most often left out of an initial fine-tuning proposal, and the one that determines whether the model remains trustworthy a year later |

The honest comparison a professional-level proposal has to make is not "fine-tuning vs. doing nothing," it's "the fully-loaded cost of training, serving, and maintaining a custom fine-tune vs. the cost of prompting or RAG against a shared base model, including engineering time either way." A narrow task where a well-crafted prompt against a shared model already clears the acceptance bar should not be fine-tuned just because the infrastructure to do so exists — the ongoing maintenance cost (Core Concept 3's retraining cadence, Core Concept 2's provenance review) is a recurring organizational cost, not a one-time project cost, and it needs to be justified against a real, sustained gap in behavior that prompting cannot close.

## Core Concept 6 — Decomposing Adoption Into Reversible Increments

Rolling out a fine-tuning capability across many product teams the same way any infrastructure capability rolls out — piloted, then expanded, never mandated wholesale:

1. **Pilot with one team, one narrow task**, ideally one where prompting has already been tried and clearly plateaued (per the junior-level checklist), so the fine-tune's value is measurable against a documented baseline, not assumed.
2. **Build the shared training pipeline and regression-suite tooling around what the pilot actually needed**, rather than speculatively building a general-purpose platform before any team has used it.
3. **Wire the data-provenance and privacy review into the pipeline as a required step for the pilot**, so the governance process is validated on one real dataset before it's asked to scale to many.
4. **Expand to additional teams only after the pilot's regression suite, rollback procedure, and retraining trigger have all been exercised at least once for real** — not just designed on paper.
5. **Track adoption as a fraction of eligible tasks actually fine-tuned, next to the cost-per-task from Core Concept 5**, so expansion decisions are made against real cost data, not enthusiasm.

Each step stays reversible: a team whose pilot fine-tune underperforms prompting can revert to prompting/RAG without the rest of the organization's plans depending on that pilot's success.

## Core Concept 7 — Outcome Measures and Exit Conditions

```yaml
program_metrics:
  task_metric_delta: "fine-tuned model's task-specific metric minus the best achievable prompting/RAG baseline for the same task"
  regression_rate: "share of deployed fine-tunes with a confirmed catastrophic-forgetting or unaddressed drift incident per quarter"
  time_to_rollback: "measured time from a confirmed regression to traffic restored on a known-good prior version"
  fully_loaded_cost_per_task: "training + serving + maintenance cost, divided by the volume of the task it serves"
exit_conditions:
  pilot_to_expansion: "pilot's task_metric_delta is positive and specific, rollback has been executed at least once in a drill, and fully_loaded_cost_per_task is documented and judged acceptable against the task's value"
  program_maturity: "regression_rate trending down, time_to_rollback consistently under the org's incident-response target, and provenance review completed for 100% of active training datasets"
```

`task_metric_delta` matters more than a raw task score, because it's the number that actually justifies fine-tuning's added cost over prompting/RAG — a fine-tune that beats prompting by a small, inconsistent margin may not be worth its ongoing maintenance burden. `regression_rate` and `time_to_rollback` are the outcome measures that prove the governance model (Core Concepts 3 and 4) is real practice, not documentation — a program with zero regressions on paper because no one is actually monitoring for drift is not a mature program, it's an unmonitored one.

## Core Concept 8 — Cross-Team Contracts

Once multiple teams depend on shared training infrastructure and a shared base model, formalize the same way an internal API is formalized:

- The platform team publishes which base models are supported, their update/deprecation schedule, and the migration path when a base model version is retired — a team's fine-tuned adapter built against a specific base version needs advance notice before that base version is removed from the shared serving infrastructure.
- A regression-suite failure is the product team's action item to fix (their task, their data); a shared-infrastructure failure (training pipeline broken, base model unavailable) is the platform team's action item — accountability follows who owns the layer where the failure originated, same as the containers-and-docker golden-image model.
- A confirmed privacy or provenance violation in a training dataset triggers the data governance team's incident process, including determining whether affected model versions need retraining or retirement — this is treated with the same severity as any other data-handling incident, not as an ML-specific exception.

## Core Concept 9 — Sustained Delivery, Not a Static Rollout

Fine-tuning capability at the organization level is never "done" — new base models are released, drift keeps happening, and new teams keep onboarding:

- **A recurring program review** against the Core Concept 7 metrics, asking explicitly whether regression rate and rollback time are actually improving, and if not, whether the gap is tooling, process adherence, or team awareness.
- **A defined path for adopting a new base model** (a new open-weight release, or a new provider's managed fine-tuning offering) into the approved list, including re-running the shared regression suite against it before any team migrates an existing fine-tune onto it.
- **New teams onboard through the same pilot-then-expand path** as the original rollout (Core Concept 6), not a shortcut, so governance and provenance review stay consistent as the program grows rather than eroding under pressure to move fast for a new team's launch date.

## Real-World Examples

- **A cost review kills a fine-tune that was winning on paper.** A team's fine-tuned classifier beats prompting by a real, measurable task-metric margin, but the fully-loaded cost accounting (Core Concept 5) shows the ongoing retraining and provenance-review burden for a low-volume task outweighs the value of the metric improvement; the team reverts to prompting and documents the decision so it isn't re-litigated without new evidence.
- **A rollback drill finds a broken procedure before an incident does.** A scheduled rollback rehearsal (Core Concept 4) reveals that the "known-good prior version" referenced in the runbook was actually deleted from the model registry during a cleanup months earlier; the gap is fixed and the drill is scheduled to repeat quarterly, catching the same class of gap before it costs real incident time.
- **A provenance review blocks a dataset, and the block is the review working correctly.** A team assembling a training set from historical support tickets is blocked by the data governance review because the tickets include unredacted account numbers that were never explicitly scoped for a model-training use case; the fix is a redaction pass before training, not an exception to the review.

## Common Mistakes

- **Centralizing every training run's approval in one platform team.** Cannot scale past a handful of teams; the review queue becomes the actual bottleneck.
- **Treating fine-tuning cost as only the training run's GPU bill.** Omits ongoing retraining, provenance review, and serving overhead, understating the real cost of the decision to fine-tune at all.
- **Skipping a rollback drill until an incident forces one.** A rollback procedure that has never been executed for real is untested, and untested procedures fail exactly when they're needed most.
- **Retraining only on a fixed calendar and never on a drift or regression signal.** Misses shifts that happen faster than the scheduled floor, reproducing the senior-level "found out from user complaints" failure at organizational scale.
- **Assuming an existing data-use agreement for a source system automatically covers using that data to train a model.** Requires a separate, explicit provenance and privacy review.
- **Measuring program success by adoption count alone**, without regression rate, rollback time, or fully-loaded cost — the same failure pattern as measuring a golden-image program by adoption percentage without patch latency.

## Apply it

1. Draft the ownership table (Core Concept 1) for your organization or a realistic one: who owns base-model selection, dataset curation, provenance review, and program health, and where the boundaries are.
2. Write the three questions from Core Concept 2 (origin, PII/consent, deletion capability) as a checklist and apply it to a specific realistic training dataset — identify what would block that dataset from being used as-is.
3. Define your retraining triggers (Core Concept 3): the scheduled floor and at least one concrete drift or regression signal, stated as a measurable threshold.
4. Write the fully-loaded cost accounting (Core Concept 5) for one fine-tuning candidate: training, serving, and ongoing maintenance cost, compared against the cost of a prompting/RAG alternative for the same task.
5. Design the rollback drill: how often it runs, what it verifies, and what "pass" looks like — before assuming the rollback procedure works.

## Verify your work

- Your ownership table assigns each layer to an owner who plausibly has the context to sustain that responsibility, not just the nearest available team.
- The provenance checklist surfaces a real potential blocker for your chosen dataset, not a rubber-stamp "looks fine."
- Your retraining triggers include at least one signal that fires before the scheduled floor would, and you can state its threshold in measurable terms.
- Your cost comparison includes ongoing maintenance cost, not just the training run's compute bill.
- You can state when your rollback drill last ran (or would run) and what evidence confirms it actually restores service, not just that a prior model artifact still exists somewhere.

## Review questions

- Why does centralizing every team's training-run approval in one platform team fail to scale, and what does the alternative ownership split preserve?
- Why is an existing data-use agreement for a source system insufficient to also cover using that data for model training?
- Why does a fixed retraining schedule alone miss regressions that a drift-signal trigger would catch earlier?
- What does a fully-loaded cost comparison include that a training-only cost estimate omits, and why does that omission matter for deciding whether to fine-tune at all?
- Why is an untested rollback procedure effectively an unknown-reliability procedure, regardless of whether a prior model version still technically exists?
